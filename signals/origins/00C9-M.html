00C9-M

"He burned their systems from the inside. Then built his own."

South London, 2087

The towers hum at night, but it’s not gentle — not the lull of traffic or the murmur of lives lived above and below. It’s the broken buzz of faulty antennae, unpatched servers, and the constant reverb of a system too stretched to sleep.

Minjae lives in the static.

Fifteen floors up in Block R9, he’s got a nest made of fibre cable, a hacked maintenance tablet, and a loopback port hidden behind a fuse panel that doesn’t technically exist. That’s where the real work happens.

“What’s your name?” the buyer asks.

“Echo.”

“What's your real name?”

Minjae doesn’t answer. Just lifts the burner tag from the solder plate, hands it over, and watches the buyer’s face flicker in the blue glow. The name changes. Age, too. Background scrubbed and replaced.

“I’ll be able to pass clearance with this?” says the buyer.

“If the scanner’s blind and the clerk’s bored? Yeah. Don’t get fancy.”

The buyer leaves with shaky hands. Minjae pockets the credits and slots the old chip into a collection of rejects. He’ll scrape the good code later, rewrite the rest.

The walls creak around him — the tower’s skin breathing in heat it can’t distribute properly. Somewhere above, someone’s arguing. Someone below slams a metal hatch. The building’s alive in the worst way: loud, unpredictable, easy to forget if you’re not paying attention.

Minjae always pays attention.

He learned early that silence is a luxury. Glitches are a language. And every system, no matter how polished, has cracks you can crawl through if you know where to apply pressure.

His mum used to call him a ghostkey. She said he could unlock anything but still act like he wasn’t there.

Back then, he thought it was a compliment.

These days, he’s not so sure.

The burner tablet pings.

INCOMING REQUEST

PATCH: ID-TAG CORRUPTION

SEVERITY: HIGH

RESPONSE NEEDED: URGENT

He swipes it open. Half the code is spaghetti. The rest is an insult to proper formatting. He mutters under his breath and starts untangling.

“I swear, if one more idiot tries to spoof an ID using a meme generator, I will personally encrypt their spleen.”

The lights flicker. Power load redistribution. Minjae doesn’t flinch. He’s already on backup — a rig he built from three different consoles and a broken visualiser. It pulses with his breath.

Below his collar, the phosphor tattoo syncs with his heartbeat.

There’s a sharp knock on the vent panel to his right. He spins, sliding the small blade from the table into his sleeve.

But it’s just a kid, barely thirteen. Nervous. Holding a badly scratched datapad like it’s contraband.

Minjae sighs. “You bricked it, didn’t you.”

The kid nods.

“Got caught installing illegal song files?”

Another nod.

Minjae holds out his hand. “You’re lucky I’m bored.”

From the next room, his mum coughs. Not the casual kind, but the dry, raw kind that drags its way up from the lungs and makes you wonder how long she’s been hiding it.

Minjae glances toward the cracked wall where her shadow flickers past. She doesn’t ask what he’s doing. She never has. She just trusts he’ll come home with enough creds to cover power and meds.

His younger sister’s probably asleep, tangled in wires she treats like treasure — plastic-coated strands she says look like “dancing light.” Minjae rewired her lamp last week to match the pattern of her breathing.

He doesn’t talk about them in the feeds. Doesn’t let their names into his systems.

But every fake ID he sells, every firewall he worms through — it’s with their faces ghosting just behind the code.

He doesn’t charge the kid.

Not this time.


They called it a “fast-track opportunity.”

What they meant was: NuYu had flagged him as viable and decided to take ownership.

He hadn’t applied. Some backend crawler must’ve scraped his debug strings off a forgotten forum and dropped his name into a database marked youth integration. One morning, a courier drone blinked at his door and spat out a glossy chipcard—white, metallic trim, NuYu's logo shimmering faintly like it knew it owned the room.

Directive 17.3B: Provisional Youth Programme.

Elegant phrasing for indentured tech labour.

Minjae arrived wearing his best hoodie and a grimace. NuYu took both.

The building gleamed, but it wasn’t clean. Too many wires, too few ethics. Everything smelled faintly burnt. The lobby piped ambient music that never resolved into a full tune, just endless, soulless loops engineered to suggest productivity.

The other kids were quieter than he expected. Blank stares, neural fields still raw from installation. One boy blinked like a buffering feed. Another girl smiled too wide and too slow. Glitchy. Someone had already installed behavioural dampeners.

They told him he’d be placed in Backend Diagnostics, Tier C. He told them they were wasting his potential. They smiled, the kind of smile that came preloaded on a corporate face.

“Your metrics indicate strong pattern recognition,” one said. “You’ll assist in system optimisations.”

Translation: he’d be elbow-deep in decaying code.

They scanned him. The relay implant came first, wired in behind his ear, the cool sting of neural gel tracing his skull. The cipher port followed two weeks later, slotted just under his collarbone. It pulsed faintly now, BPM-synced, a constant reminder that even his heartbeat was plugged into something.

Minjae started work in silence and observation. He learned more by not speaking. His sector handled data sweeps, mostly boring—logs, diagnostics, broken lines of failed memory exports.

But every so often, something surfaced.

Old employee IDs that shouldn’t exist. Files that blinked out halfway through review. Audio logs tagged “sim tests” that sounded far too human.

He flagged one of them out of pure curiosity. 

His clearance dropped by three tiers the next morning.

A week later, his datapad rebooted mid-use. All logs wiped, cleaned as if he’d never touched it.

Except—they missed one file.

His file.

Looped vocal test. Experimental overlay. The voice cracked in places, faltered like it had once belonged to someone real. Not rendered. Remembered.

That was the moment he understood what NuYu really did with the people it couldn’t use.

They weren’t killed. They were overwritten.

He sat in the breakroom that night, fingers still stained with wire-solder and dust, watching the others. Teenagers with blink rates optimised for compliance. He wondered how many had been like him once—angry, too sharp, unwilling to stay quiet. He wondered what NuYu had taken from them to make them like this.

Minjae wasn’t ready to let that happen to him.

So he started backing himself up.


The server room was always too cold. NuYu said it preserved machine efficiency. Minjae said it was so no one could feel anything.

He waited until the midnight shift swap, just long enough for the patrol AI to glitch like it always did when the building tried to update itself and run security scripts at the same time. Budget cuts. Bad code. Minjae had warned them about it in a report three months ago.

They’d flagged it low-priority.

He walked in like he belonged there. Because he did. That was the beauty of it—NuYu had trained him to breach itself.

He bypassed the node locks with his own ID. Paused only once, hand hovering over the authentication pad, wondering if this made him a traitor or just ahead of schedule.

The door opened with a hiss that felt like breath held too long.

Inside, the mainframe pulsed like a buried heart. Blue-white light across rows of servers, each one humming with the memory of someone else’s silence. Files tagged. People redacted. Voices replaced. Code stacked on code stacked on absence.

Minjae pulled the drive he’d pre-loaded—his own scripts. They weren’t elegant, but they were effective. A virus dressed like an audit, something the system wouldn’t notice until it was already bleeding.

He slotted it into the main terminal and it immediately asked for confirmation.

He didn’t blink.

But the system fought back. As soon as the data spike began to burn through the core registry, the override kicked in, locking down access, superheating the ports.

Then the drive exploded in a flare of blue sparks.

Minjae threw himself back, but not fast enough. His right hand caught the worst of it—scorched along the knuckles and palm, skin splitting open with the smell of melted poly-fibre and flesh. He bit down a scream and stumbled, half-blind, but still smiling.

Because the lights were dying, one by one.

The servers were going offline.

It was working.

He grabbed the edge of the console with his left hand, dragging himself upright. Blood smeared across the glass. No alarms—he’d silenced those too. Just the hum of systems shutting down, data collapsing in on itself like a star that refused to shine for anyone but the truth.

He staggered to the exit, hand cradled close, teeth grit through pain and adrenaline.

Behind him, Node B6 went dark. Permanently.

They’d say it was sabotage.

He preferred the term: extraction.


Minjae didn’t remember how he got down into The Ghost Lines, only that he hurt. Everything hurt.

His hand was wrapped in a stolen thermal cloth, soaked through. The pain had gone sharp, then numb. That was probably bad.

London above was still chasing ghosts, while deep below, Minjae became one.

The Ghost Lines weren’t a place, not really. More like a rumour of safety, layered in static and sewer steam. You didn’t get invited. You got desperate.

Eventually he found a ladder. He remembered blood on the rungs—his blood. He remembered cold steel against his ribs, and then a tunnel that didn’t end so much as forget to close.

He woke once in a nest of wires and broken monitors. Once in a hydro duct. Once in the dark, heartbeat synced to the pulsing of distant relay lights.

No name. No ID. No registry.

NuYu had scrubbed all of it.

They tried to reduce him to a redacted footnote, a simple line of code commented out, but they’d missed something.

He’d already seeded fragments of himself across the net—backups hidden in abandoned media servers, lyrics embedded in glitchwave files, fragments of identity nested in old digital graffiti. Anyone trying to rewrite him would find themselves looped back to truth.


In the quiet days—if you could call them that—he started to compose again. You couldn’t call it music, not yet. It was just phrases. Raw, spliced thoughts. Code that almost rhymed.

“They erased my employee ID. So I gave myself a new one.”

He stitched a new ID out of memory: 00C9-M. It was his great-grandfather’s once. His granddad had once helped bring a system to its knees with nothing but conviction and a typewriter.

He wrote it in phosphor across a data vault wall. Just once. Just to see it.

00C9-M.

The paint flickered, BPM-synced to the rumble of something alive deeper in the tunnels.

And slowly, verse by verse, the rumour began.

Encrypted tracks started leaking onto public channels; untraceable, voice-tagged, laced with fury and ghost-code. Snatches of truth inside distortion. They couldn’t find him, but they could hear him.

The Hollow noticed.

They didn’t ask who he was, just if he could please keep going.


At first, it wasn’t meant to be a track.

He’d cracked the terminal out of habit—wires patched with chewing gum foil, screen stuttering like it knew the end was coming. No surveillance. No net uplink. Just a forgotten slice of nowhere, perfect for vanishing.

He was only running diagnostics. Clearing space. Looping old files. That’s all.

But then the beatpad came alive—low pulse, wobbly tempo, nothing finished.

And he thought: fine. One more file. One last purge.

So he started talking.

Not rapping or decoding. Just… spilling, line after line like a pressure valve giving out. Glitches tangled in breath, syllables stuttering raw.

“You ever look in a mirror and wonder if your name’s still yours?”

“You ever glitch so hard you think maybe they left something in you?”

He didn’t structure or loop it, just let the words fall glitchy and human, rage threaded through regret, pride edged with static. The chorus was scratched in rough with half-dead synth keys. The audio clipped on the high end. One of the verses broke halfway through because his hand still shook when he pushed too hard.

He kept it all. No edits. No encryption.

He didn’t expect anyone to find it.

And if they did?

Well.

It was just a note.

That’s what he told himself.

But something in the tremor of his fingers said otherwise.


It was too quiet, even for him.

Minjae had rerouted the security grid hours ago. No cams. No scans. No passive motion pings. The only thing live down here was the soft flicker of emergency strips, bleeding pale light through a cracked wall panel.

That—and the notebook.

It wasn’t even his. Just a leftover grid-paper ledger from some old Hollow stockpile, the kind rebels used to log equipment or ration shifts. Someone had scrawled “USELESS” on the front in marker. That made him keep it.

He’d burned through the first few pages fast. Angry lines. Scrambled half-rhymes. Code fragments mixed with lyrics. Stuff too raw to spit out loud.

He flipped through now. Hands steady, but scarred—skin still tight where the fire had taken him. His knuckles were mapped with memory.

Some pages held verses. Some held names he swore he’d never forget. Most were crossed out, as if that would stop the memories bleeding through.

He stared at one line:

“They said I wasn’t essential. So I rewrote the system.”

Minjae shut the book and crossed the room, climbing onto the support rail, his fingers slipping into the exposed vent just above the main coolant pipe. A perfect dead zone. Too small for drones. Too hidden for scans.

He shoved the notebook deep into the ductwork and taped it with blackout foil, then sealed the vent with a loose grate.

Out of sight.

If they ever found him—if they ever caught him—it wouldn’t matter.

The words would still be here.

Waiting.


He saw them before they saw him.

Two figures walking in the opposite direction, maybe fifteen paces away. One tall, broad-shouldered, with a soldier’s gait—ex-military, judging by the augments. The other was leaner, all limbs and mismatched stride, hoodie glowing faint from some kind of embedded fibre. Talking with his hands. Laughing at his own joke.

Minjae kept to the shadows, but his eyes tracked every move.

They didn’t stop or speak to him, but the one in the hoodie glanced sideways as they passed and offered him a huge, beaming smile. It was so out of the blue that Minjae could only stare back.


A few hours later, Minjae trekked through the same tunnel on his way back to his hideout, and there was that lean guy again. Same hoodie, same damn grin, as if he’d been waiting.

Minjae slowed, aware of the knife tucked into his pocket.

The lean guy tilted his head like he was listening to a frequency no one else could hear.

“You’re the guy who’s been splicing solo feeds,” he said. Not an accusation. Not even a question.

Minjae raised an eyebrow. “And you’re the guy who keeps overloading the channel limit.”

The guy grinned. “Only when it sounds better that way.”

They stood there for a beat too long.

“I’m Kairo,” the guy said.

Minjae ran through the pros and cons of giving up his name to this small, energetic weirdo, but Kairo’s face was so damn earnest.

“Minjae,” he said.

Kairo seemed pleased by that. “Cool. Cool.” He beamed.

Minjae found his openness uncomfortable, so he shrugged, turned, and kept walking. 

Kairo didn’t follow him, but his voice echoed after him down the tunnel. “I liked that last payload. The one that looped at 140 BPM. You layered something real in there.”

Minjae didn’t respond, but his mouth twitched, just slightly.

Not a smile.

Not yet.


Minjae doesn’t go into the room. He could if he wanted to—no one’s guarding the door, and it’s not like he doesn’t know the access ports by now. But he’s still wary, so instead, he sets up shop in the tunnel just beyond.

Old cable spools for a seat. Wires overhead like vines. A dusty terminal with a cracked screen he patches together just enough to parse code on. That’s his perch, just close enough to hear when someone plays a loop out loud. Close enough to pick up the hum of beat sync through the concrete.

He tells himself it’s just a better location with more network access and less signal interference.

He doesn’t tell himself that he’s waiting.

Kairo walks past once, holding three energy cells and a snack bar in his teeth. He nods like they’ve always known each other.

The silver-haired guy—Soahn? That’s what Minjae heard Kairo call him—doesn’t say anything. He just reroutes one of the external leads to power the terminal near Minjae’s corner.

The one Minjae is pretty sure isn’t human—Rayne—passes through like a ghost, unreadable eyes flicking toward him, then away again.

The big guy, Onyx, never speaks but there’s always a glance, like he’s checking Minjae’s location, making sure of something.

Minjae listens to them build a track. He hears them argue over compression settings and vocal mix and lyrical tone.

He doesn’t offer feedback.

But he does fix a bug in their code overnight, leaving a short patch note in the logs signed with nothing but 00C9-M.


Minjae steps over the threshold.

He doesn’t announce himself—he never has and doesn’t plan to start now—but he doesn’t turn away either. He stands inside the door, his shoulder against the frame, his eyes adjusting to the dim neon flicker of rig lights and beatpads.

He takes it in.

Soahn is perched near the far wall, working on some sort of neural relay. The delicate kind. He’s precise and thoughtful, all long fingers and unspoken calculations. He doesn’t look up, but Minjae can feel it—the quiet awareness. Like Soahn’s got an internal radar that logs presence before sound.

Rayne is still. Freakishly still. Sitting cross-legged near a speaker stack, head tilted like he’s listening to something no one else can hear. There’s something fractured about him, like he’s here, but also not. Synthetic, sure, but not hollow. Minjae watches the rhythm of Rayne’s breathing. It doesn’t match the track playing. That tells him a lot.

Onyx is leaning against a console, arms crossed, expression unreadable. Military. Augmented. The kind of presence that doesn’t flinch unless it needs to. Minjae can already tell: this one watches everyone. Probably clocked him the second he entered. Doesn’t say a word, but Minjae knows he’s sizing him up, calculating variables. Fine. Let him.

And then there’s Kairo.

Kairo, who’s in the middle of rewiring a beatpad with no tools except his teeth and a multitool that’s mostly duct tape. He’s got a shock of wires coming out of his jacket like they’re part of his nervous system. Neon glint in his eyes. The kind of chaos that’s either genius or self-destructing.

Kairo notices him instantly.

“You lost?” he asks, flipping a loose wire behind his ear. “Or are you finally here to tell us how we’re doing it all wrong?”

Minjae lifts a brow. “Depends. You planning to sync that vocal track with the tempo or just keep torturing it?”

Kairo lights up. “See, I knew you were a fan.”

He tosses a datapad across the room—reckless, casual. Minjae catches it easily with his good hand.

It’s a track skeleton. Beat markers. Placeholder rhymes that aren’t half-bad. The kind of layout someone made while half-asleep on synthesised sugar and spite.

“If you’re gonna loiter in our tunnels,” Kairo says, “might as well do something useful.”

Minjae eyes the datapad. “This is a mess.”

“So fix it,” Kairo grins. “Unless you’re scared.”

That grin is irritating. And persuasive.

Minjae crosses the room. Drops into the nearest half-broken swivel chair like he owns it. Doesn’t look at any of them again, just pulls up the audio layers and starts working. Fast. Efficient. A few rewrites. One total deletion. The beat steadies. Lyrics already start to form in his mind.

“Don’t breathe on me while I’m thinking,” he mutters.

“No promises,” says Kairo.

Off in the corner, Soahn smiles. Just barely. Like this was inevitable.

Rayne shifts his posture, just slightly. Watching. Listening.

Onyx doesn’t move.

And Minjae… stays.


Later, once the others have drifted out or gone to recharge ports or wherever it is this glitch-gathered band of misfits disappears to, Minjae settles back into the chair. It’s just him and the beatpad, and the faint glow of the console still pulsing with the track he half-built.

He lets his head droop for a while, a little tired from all the social stuff, just enjoying the faint hum of the equipment. Then barefoot steps approach, quiet as static.

Minjae doesn’t look up. “If you’re here to ask for a bridge section, you’ll have to bribe me.”

“I’m not.” Soahn sits, not quite beside him but still close. The kind of distance that says I’ll wait until you’re ready, but I’m not going anywhere.

They sit in silence for a while, and weirdly, it’s not uncomfortable.

Then Soahn says, “You write like someone who was supposed to be erased.”

Minjae stills, unable to answer.

“You leave space for grief. And then fill it with fire.”

Minjae presses one hand flat to the console. The burn scars along his right fingers catch the glow. They’re healing, but visible. Always visible.

Soahn doesn’t ask about them. He just says, “They almost got you, didn’t they?”

Minjae closes his eyes.

“…Almost.”

Soahn nods. “Then we’re the same.”

Minjae looks at him for the first time. Really looks. There’s something unreadable in Soahn’s gaze—not pity, not curiosity. Something quieter. Mutual recognition, like a mirror held at the right angle.

“You don’t pry,” Minjae says.

Soahn shrugs. “You don’t hide.”

Another pause.

Then Soahn stands and says, “That track you started? Keep building it. I think it remembers you.”

And he walks away.

Minjae watches him go.

And for the first time since the fire, he thinks: maybe he doesn’t have to burn everything down to leave a mark.
