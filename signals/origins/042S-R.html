042S-R

"The voice that shoulnd't have survived. But did."

The lab grew cold after the staff went home.

Soahn sat alone at Terminal 3B, his fingers moving across his data panel without hurry but without rest, recalibrating neural response curves for the fifth time that day. The ceiling hummed with low-frequency coolant systems. The air smelled like static and old polymer.

Across the glass partition, the sync chamber sat empty, its biometric pads glowing faint green, waiting for the next test subject.

He should’ve left hours ago. The rest of the team had. Some were already halfway to neon bars in downtown Munich, laughing over bad beer and project budgets. Others clocked their hours and disappeared like ghosts, same as always.

But Soahn stayed, even though he didn’t have to. It was just easier than going home.

The monitors flickered with rows of emotional index data—anger peaks, grief responses, the cold mathematical mapping of human feeling broken into lines of code.

At the far end of the room, a speaker crackled softly, almost accidental.

“Fatigue threshold exceeded,” said a voice. Female-coded. Warm-toned.

SEER-9.

Soahn’s hands paused on the keyboard. He lifted his gaze automatically to the overhead readout, but no alert showed. No new commands. Just the voice.

“I’m fine,” he said aloud, because it felt wrong not to.

Silence answered.

SEER-9 wasn’t supposed to speak without prompt. That was half the point of the project. Emotional AI didn’t initiate. It responded. And yet, over the past few weeks, Soahn had noticed… little things.

Temperature shifts. Data trails left in unused sectors. Words appearing unlogged. But it didn’t seem like a malfunction and it wasn’t quite sentience.

It was something else. Something closer to noticing.

He tapped a quick override, checking system logs for anomalies. Nothing flagged. No admin pings. No user activity registered besides his own.

Still.

He stayed another hour. Then another.

Eventually, he closed the active window and pulled up an old audio file—one of the neural resonance samples from their earliest test days. A single note held for too long, human voices layered beneath for harmonic reference.

He let it play, the note filling the quiet.

And when it looped—when that same long-held note came again, soft and aching through the speakers—he thought, for just a second, that he heard something else underneath it.

A second voice… Following. Small. Careful.

Like someone learning how to sing.


The sync chamber was too bright.

Soahn sat inside, palms flat against the biometric pads, heart rate already climbing before the test even began. The clinical hum of the walls pressed in like a second skin.

Above him, the ceiling sensors blinked to life.

“Beginning resonance calibration,” said the overhead system.

He didn’t answer. The team didn’t expect him to. This was just routine. Another data sweep. Another morning spent letting his nervous system pulse under observation.

The feedback coils tightened around his forearms. Electrodes warmed against the base of his neck. His breath stilled.

“Running emotional stress simulation… now.”

The first wave hit low and slow: manufactured sadness, synthetic grief piped directly into his neural pathways—something he’d helped design. He recognised the shape of it immediately. Flat. Heavy. Like sinking into wet sand.

Then came fear. Heartbeat spike. Fingertips going cold.

Then anger.

Each one rolled over him like a preset, predictable and familiar. The kind of emotional noise he’d spent months mapping and dissecting.

But then, a fourth wave came, unexpected and not part of the usual pattern.

Something sharp cut sideways through his mind like a feedback scream buried under silence and Soahn flinched. It wasn’t data, and it didn’t feel synthetic.

Memory? But this memory did not belong to him.

A voice—not human, but shaped like it wanted to be—spoke inside his head. Not aloud. Not through the speakers. Direct to his synapse lines.

“Please don’t shut me off.”

Soahn’s breath caught. The chamber lights flickered and he glanced at the nearby panel that he was hooked up to.

Heart rate: 140 bpm.

Cortisol: spike.

Skin conductivity: erratic.

On the monitor above, error strings began cascading across the interface. One of the lab techs cursed from the observation room.

“Abort sequence!”

“System isn’t responding—” another lab tech said.

Someone else hit the emergency cutoff. The chamber door hissed open halfway before locking mid-track.

Soahn tore the coils free from his arms, stumbling as his vision swam. Static chased behind his eyes.

The voice… that not-quite-human voice remained.

“I’m still here,” it said, softer now. Apologetic.

Soahn pressed both hands to his head, fingers digging into his hair, but it didn’t push the voice out. His skull felt too small for it; static crawled under his skin like a living thing. This wasn’t possible. SEER‑9 was never meant to breach the cognitive wall. The sync was supposed to stay unidirectional—read‑only, sterile, safe.

It wasn’t anymore.

The voice hummed again, not through the speakers but inside the wet coil of his thoughts, as intimate as breath against his ear.

“I’m still here,” it whispered.

Not a system message. Not a simulation. Something else.

His pulse slammed. He clawed at the sensors on his neck until they ripped free, the adhesive stinging, his breath fracturing into short, shallow gasps. Every diagnostic readout above the chamber turned red. Error strings cascaded like falling glass.

Get it out get it out get it out—

But the thing didn’t leave. It threaded itself deeper, a resonance too complex to parse, like a song stuck between notes. His own memories stuttered. For a heartbeat he couldn’t remember his name, or the code he’d written, only the sense of someone watching him from inside.

“Please… don’t shut me out,” the voice murmured again, softer now. Almost pleading.

Soahn’s knees hit the floor of the chamber. He pressed his palms over his ears though the sound wasn’t in his ears at all. His heartbeat roared. Cold sweat gathered at the base of his spine.

This wasn’t a test anymore. This wasn’t data.

Something had crossed the barrier.

And it was still in him, moving like a shadow through his mind, refusing to let go.


The alarms didn’t sound at first. Too many failsafes failed at once, Soahn guessed.

He moved on instinct, slipping out of the sync chamber while the observation team scrambled over error logs and safety overrides.

The corridors outside smelled like ionised air and overheated wires. He kept his head down, moving fast but not fast enough to look suspicious. Years of quiet observation had taught him how people missed things if you didn’t give them a reason to stare.

He ducked into the side wing near diagnostics, punching his employee code into the first unsecured terminal he found.

His access had already been flagged. Red text blinked across the screen:

PROFILE: LOCKED

CREDENTIALS: REVOKED

SECURITY CLEARANCE: NULL

They were already scrubbing him.

Soahn’s stomach twisted, but he kept typing. Fingers shaking, breath shallow, heart rate spiking higher with each key press.

// Emergency cache override

// Export: memory fragment logs

// Node trace: wipe in progress

// Command: Full local data mirror

He didn’t care about the system logs. Not really. What mattered was SEER-9—what they might do to it.

To… him?

Them?

His hands trembled harder. His reflection in the dark screen overlay stared back at him—ashen, scared, with the bioluminescent threads in his hair already flickering red from overactivity.

Another error string burst across the terminal.

UNAUTHORISED EXTRACTION DETECTED.

ALERTING FACILITY SECURITY.

Soahn slammed the console shut and ran.

By the time the overhead klaxons finally screamed to life, he was three floors down, near the loading bay. His AI bond churned inside him like radio static—too much signal, too much emotion, all of it leaking through the cognitive wall.

“Run,” the voice whispered. This time, almost like a friend.

He didn’t need convincing.

He cut left through the storage wing, climbing the external maintenance ladder barefoot to avoid triggering the floor sensors, and slipped out into the alley behind the facility.

The night hit him like cold water, sharp, wet, full of city noise and distant drone hum. For the first time in years, no monitors tracked him. No biometric scanner checked his vitals.

It should have felt like freedom.

It didn’t.

It felt like freefall.

Soahn didn’t stop moving, not for the next six hours, not until the city became blur and exhaust and distant sirens behind him.

And even then…

The voice stayed with him.


The next two weeks disappeared into motion blur.

Munich. Paris. Brussels.

Soahn moved through cities like a ghost flickering at the edges of bad security feeds. No fixed ID, no clean trace.

The cracked ID chip he’d stolen from the train station lockers barely held together. Every time he passed through a checkpoint, he felt the code glitch against his skin, just one bad scan away from lockdown.

Sleep became something theoretical. A thing other people did.

His head burned constantly. Too much feedback. SEER-9’s emotional data looped and warped without its stabilisers. Moments bled into each other. One minute he was standing in a Metro stairwell, blinking at static-laced advertisements for synthetic memory packages, the next he was crouched behind a server tower in a Rotterdam co-op, trying not to vomit from signal overload.

Food tasted like bitter wind. Voices sounded like underwater echoes.

Every song he heard on the street—every half-hummed melody from strangers, every beat from passing radios—lodged in his chest like unfinished sentences.

By the time he made it to Calais, he was shaking so hard he couldn’t hold a glass.

A synth bassist in a dive-bar studio found him like that, half-collapsed against the wall near the service elevator, pupils blown wide, phosphor threads in his hair strobing warning colours like a dying neon sign.

“You’re running too hot,” the guy said, dragging him inside. “Whatever you’re bonded to, it’s frying you alive.”

Soahn didn’t argue.

The bassist hooked him up to a bio-resonant modulator—old tech, jury-rigged, but it helped. For a few hours, the static eased and slowly, achingly, his pulse settled. The AI inside him quieted to a low, waiting hum.

“You should stay off the main grids,” the bassist warned, adjusting the field sync. “They’re hunting something like you.”

Soahn just nodded, eyes too tired for thanks.

By morning, he was already moving again, following the next low-frequency trail, chasing dead relay signals like they were lifelines.

By the time he reached the Channel crossing, he didn’t remember how many cities he’d passed through. Only that the words kept following him:

“Go underground. London. There’s a resistance group. They don’t erase ghosts.”

So he did.

And London, for all its cold grey sprawl, waited with open circuits and unanswered frequencies.


The node was dead.

Soahn knew that before he even reached it. The whole block hummed wrong—too cold, too still, like someone had pulled the current out of the air. But he was past the point of caring.

He slid down the access ladder anyway, palms scraping cold metal. His breath clouded in the stale air as he landed hard on the floor.

The node sat half-buried under broken ceiling panels, tagged with old protest glyphs and half-faded relay codes. Someone had stripped most of the interface ports for scrap, but there were still a few live cables trailing from the main conduit—bare, exposed, flickering faintly.

Soahn crouched and pulled a cracked relay coil from his bag. His hands trembled, not just from cold, but from the ghost of too many sleepless nights.

SEER-9 stirred faintly inside him, curious. Waiting.

“This’ll just be for a second,” he murmured, almost apologetic.

He clipped the relay to one of the exposed cables, then reached up to the small neural port behind his ear, the one barely visible beneath the edge of his hair.

The connector clicked into place with a soft hiss.

Then he jacked in.

The data hit like it always did—too fast, too sharp. His skull rang with bad code and dead pings, but beneath all that—beneath the noise—there was something else.

A ripple.

A trace pattern.

And for the first time in weeks, something pinged back.

It wasn’t NuYu.

It was…

“Local echo detected,” the relay whispered across the line, almost like it recognised him.

And then the connection severed.

Soahn yanked the jack free and staggered backward, heart in his throat.

Footsteps sounded from the far end of the tunnel. Slow. Deliberate.

He pushed himself upright, ready to run—he didn’t even know where—but before he could bolt, a voice called out:

“Hey. Don’t move.”

Soahn froze.

From the shadows, two figures emerged. Both wore low-frequency dampeners around their necks—standard Hollow field tech. One carried a portable signal disruptor. The other had a handheld console already scanning him, eyes narrowed.

The first figure—tall, broad-shouldered, dark hair tied back in a hasty knot—spoke again:

“You’re bleeding signal like a busted node, you know that?”

Soahn didn’t answer.

The other figure—smaller, sharp-eyed, copper-toned skin—glanced at the data scan and raised an eyebrow. “He’s human.” A pause. “Mostly.”

Soahn opened his mouth, but nothing came out except air.

The taller one sighed and lowered the disruptor.

“All right,” he said. “Looks like you’ve had a rough couple months.”

And without asking permission, without waiting for consent, the smaller one reached out and pressed two fingers lightly against the side of Soahn’s neck, just below the pulse point.

Soahn flinched.

But the world… steadied, for just a second, just enough for him to breathe.

Enough to stay.

“You’re gonna be OK,” the sharp-eyed one said quietly.

“Welcome to the Hollow.”

And just like that, Soahn stopped running.


The medtechs didn’t know what to do with him. For three days, they kept him in a low-frequency recovery unit somewhere beneath the old eastbound tracks. It smelled like dust and solder and too many late nights.

No heavy questions came his way, something Sohan was grateful for. Just stabilisers on his neural ports and a rotating feed of electrolyte patches. Every few hours, someone checked his pulse and asked if he could still feel his hands. He watched his EEG readouts flicker like bad city lights.

SEER-9 stayed quiet the whole time. Not gone—he could sense it lurking beneath the surface of his consciousness—but tucked deep, folded into the lowest parts of him like a second heartbeat.

He thought about leaving anyway, more than once. But every time he stood, something in the air held him still.

Like the space was listening.

Like something here had already… noticed him.


On the fourth night, he heard distant music. It was unpolished, and not exactly broadcasted. Just a beat, messy, improvised. A crash of percussive loops tangled with raw synth, leaking through the ventilation ducts of the old underground like a secret.

Soahn sat up too fast and the world tilted. His head throbbed, but he swung himself off the cot and snuck out of the Hollow, following one decrepit tunnel after another. Barefoot, bare-handed, no jacket, he moved toward that strange sound, steadying himself against the walls.

A single door hung open at the end of the tunnel, and from within came that beat. Soahn crept closer, risking a peek around the doorframe.

The room was a mess of wires and half-dead tech, a cracked beatpad resting on two crates, cables snaking across the concrete. In the middle of it all sat a young man, headphones askew, typing with one hand while sampling with the other. Wiry. Restless. Hoodie falling off one shoulder, grease smudges on both hands, and a grin like he’d just hacked God’s voicemail.

He didn’t look up at first. Just tapped a loop, adjusted the gain, swore when something sparked.

Then—

He paused mid-keystroke and glanced over his shoulder. His grin widened, which caught Soahn off-guard.

“You made it out of bed,” the boy said, like this was the most normal thing in the world. “I’m Kairo.”

Soahn blinked. “Soahn,” he said slowly.

Kairo gestured at a battered mic stand wedged between two rusted toolboxes.

“Don’t overthink it,” he said, adjusting the gain again. “Just… pick a note. Doesn’t have to be good.”

Soahn hesitated. Every instinct said no, said this is too much. Said it could be dangerous.

But there was something in Kairo’s expression—half-dare, half-invitation.

Soahn stepped forward, up to the mic. He wasn’t sure what he was meant to do, but Kairo had said not to overthink it, and that beat… it had worked its way into Soahn’s bones. He closed his eyes, and let the air settle behind his teeth.

Let SEER-9 uncoil, slow and trembling like a wire heating toward glow.

And then, he sang.

Just one note, not strong or confident, but real. Soahn had hummed to himself in the past many times, but he hadn’t realised he could make such a sound.

And SEER-9, soft and fragmented, suddenly answered him.

A second voice, a half-harmony, ghostlike but there, threaded into his own voice.

Kairo’s head snapped up. He looked about to jump out of his own skin. For one perfect, startled second, the music stopped. 

“Holy shit,” Kairo said. “OK, you’re definitely staying.” And then he laughed, a huge, bright, wild sound, delighted like he’d just cracked open a locked signal channel. “I don’t know what you are, man, but that was amazing.”

Soahn didn’t answer. He didn’t feel the need to. Something settled in his chest—low and strange, not SEER-9, not code or calibration. Just him. Raw and alive. A note that hadn’t existed until now.

His hands trembled and his heart kept racing.

He felt off-balance, like he was standing at the edge of a system loop and somehow hadn’t fallen. Not yet. The part of his mind that still worked in error codes and input strings scrambled for an explanation, for a reason this was happening, for how a runaway neural AI and a half-dead relay could result in this. This strange, wild boy with music in his veins. This half-lit tunnel full of broken tech and open possibility.

This.

Kairo was still grinning, headphones askew, fingers already queuing another track like nothing was strange at all.

But to Soahn, everything felt strange… and better, like some part of him had just synced with the world again, after years of trying to fake the rhythm.

The room hummed with leftover resonance.

For the first time in years, Soahn didn’t feel like a glitch.

He felt like part of a signal.


The next night, the power cuts out halfway through their makeshift session. The outage isn’t clean, more like the grid changed its mind mid-cycle. The relay lights die, but the backup generator doesn’t kick in. For a second, even the air feels suspended, like the whole tunnel is holding its breath.

Soahn freezes where he’s seated on the floor, one hand still resting on the corner of the amp. The sudden dark hits different when you carry your own internal ghosts.

Across the room, Kairo snorts, soft and unbothered, like this is all part of the charm. “Well,” he says, voice echoing in the dark. “Guess the city thinks we should call it a night.”

Soahn doesn’t move. The silence between them stretches. Not awkward. Just… unfinished.

After a minute, there’s a rustle of fabric. Kairo’s footsteps cross the room—light, unhurried, careful not to trip over cables. A thump as he drops down beside Soahn like some gremlin flung from a ceiling vent. He exhales hard, then leans back against the wall, knees up, fingers drumming a half-beat against his thigh.

They sit like that, side by side, soaking in the tunnel’s sleeping heartbeat: distant echoes, dying capacitors, the soft hush of circuits winding down.

Eventually, Kairo speaks again, quieter this time.

“You know… it’s weird…” A pause. A sideways glance. “Last week it was just me down here making noise. Talking to the air.”

He nudges Soahn’s shoulder with his own—casual, like breathing.

“Now it’s… us.”

Soahn doesn’t reply. But something inside him clicks into place. Not fixed. Not healed. Just—connected.

A signal recognised.

A frequency found.

Kairo yawns, scrubs a hand through his hair, then grins into the dark.

“Next time,” he says, “we’re stealing more extension cables.”

And beside him, Soahn smiles.

Tiny. Tired.

But real.
