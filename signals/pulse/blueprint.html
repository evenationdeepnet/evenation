Blueprint

Minjae shoved his chair back with a groan. “That’s it, I’m deleting the whole track. We’re starting again from zero.” The last loop cracked and fizzed into silence, leaving the bunker thrumming with leftover sound.

Soahn didn’t even look up. “You say that every time it clips by half a second.”

“That’s because it keeps clipping by half a second.” Minjae jabbed a finger at the waveform, which continued glitching stubbornly. “I swear it’s doing it just to spite me.”

Kairo laughed, loud, bright, and just this side of unhinged. “It definitely is. I’ve been teaching it sentience for weeks.”

“Don’t joke about that,” Soahn said quickly, horror in his voice. “Please.”

Across the room, Rayne sat cross-legged on the floor with a coil of cable draped loosely over his hands, coaxing a soft repeating tone from the line. Every vibration answered the one before it, delicate and precise, like a private conversation spoken in frequencies.

Onyx, standing with one shoulder against the pillar by the door, watched the room with that settled, unreadable stillness of his. Eyes dark. Arms folded. The kind of posture that looked like stillness but was actually attention.

Minjae scrubbed his face with both hands. “I need caffeine before I destroy something.”

“You mean before I destroy something,” Kairo corrected. “Your destruction rate is amateur at best.”

“Remind me why we keep you again?” Minjae muttered.

“For the entertainment value,” Kairo said cheerfully. “And because you’d all be lost without me.”

Rayne’s head tilted at that, gaze unfocused, as if he was listening to something none of them could hear.

Soahn caught Kairo’s eye and jerked his chin toward the door. “We’re done for tonight. If we don’t sleep, we’re going to start hallucinating.”

“Speak for yourself,” Kairo said. “I transcended that stage three hours ago.”

Minjae gathered his datapads. “If I dream about corrupted layers again, I’m taking it out on all of you.”

“Noted,” Onyx murmured, pushing off from the pillar. His voice held that dry, understated humour only the others ever caught.

There was a small flurry of movement—chairs scraped, cables gathered, Soahn herding Minjae out with a hand on his shoulder, Onyx behind them. Rayne drifted toward the corridor without a word, quiet as smoke, his thoughts clearly somewhere else again.

Kairo stood for a moment in the sudden hush, staring at the empty floor where Rayne had been. The space felt wrong. Too open and quiet, like a sound cut abruptly instead of fading naturally.

Onyx hesitated in the doorway, glancing back once.

“You coming?” he asked.

“In a minute,” Kairo said, trying to sound casual. “Just cleaning up.”

Onyx gave a slow, steady nod and stepped out of sight.

And then the silence settled in properly.

Kairo let out a breath that wasn’t steady at all. He moved quickly, almost frantically, dragging a storage crate closer, pulling out pieces he definitely shouldn’t have—coils of wire, solder tools, a half-built device with exposed circuitry and more ambition than structural integrity.

He muttered under his breath, tapping rhythm on metal with quick, restless fingers. Sparks jumped as the solder hissed across contact points. His shoulders hunched low over the work, as if trying to protect something fragile from the quiet.

The bunker listened.

And down the corridor, soft almost to nothing, footsteps returned.


Kairo didn’t look up at first, afraid to show too much of what he was working on. He pretended he hadn’t heard the footsteps or the soft shift of air as someone stopped in the doorway. He kept soldering, fast and messy, the metal shaking slightly in his grip.

“You’re going to burn through the board like that,” Onyx said.

Kairo paused. “Thought you’d gone to sleep.”

“I did,” Onyx replied. “Didn’t take.”

A beat. The overhead lights hummed.

Kairo set the tool down with hands that weren’t steady anymore. “You don’t have to babysit me.”

Onyx moved into the room, steps slow and measured. He didn’t come close—just far enough that Kairo could feel the presence rather than the distance. “I’m not.”

“Sure,” Kairo said with a laugh that sounded too loud to be real. “Because everyone always comes back to watch me melt down at two in the morning over half-broken tech.”

“Kairo.”

One word. Low but not sharp—careful, like something fragile underfoot.

Kairo swallowed hard. The silence stretched thin as wire.

He finally turned, leaning back against the table to keep from sliding straight to the floor.

Onyx stood a few paces away, framed by the low bunker light, shoulders squared, posture loose but ready, dark hair falling unusually messy as though he’d run a hand through it one too many times. There was a faint line between his brows, tension he hadn’t quite managed to hide.

He didn’t push, just waited for Kairo to find his voice again.

“It’s stupid,” Kairo finally said. “I know it’s stupid. I just… I needed to build something that could track him properly. Not guesses. Not waiting. Not hoping he’ll come back.”

Onyx didn’t speak.

Kairo pressed the heel of his hand to his forehead. “I just—the last time he went quiet that long, I thought—” He cut himself off, jaw tight. “I thought maybe he wasn’t coming back. Maybe he’d decided he was better off without us.”

The ache in the room shifted shape, becoming something heavier.

Onyx stepped closer, not touching, but close enough that the distance changed. Close enough that the silence felt full and not empty.

“He wouldn’t leave,” he said quietly.

“You don’t know that.”

“I do.” Onyx paused, as if startled by his own conviction. “I know it because I know him. He’s like me, and I’m still here.”

Kairo blinked fast. “Onyx—”

“I know what it’s like to lose things,” Onyx continued, steady but not cold. “We all do. We wouldn’t do that to each other.”

The air trembled with everything he didn’t say.

“So if you want to build something to reach him,” Onyx finished, “you don’t do it alone.”

Kairo let out a shaky laugh, somewhere between relief and breaking. “You’re really going to help me build a tracking device for a man who glitches out of reality like a ghost.”

Onyx’s mouth tugged into the smallest half-smile. “I’ve done stranger things.”

“Like choosing to hang out with me?”

“Exactly.”

Something in Kairo’s chest loosened. Not gone, but gentler.

He slid the semi-built rig across the table, metal scraping softly across the surface. “All right then, soldier boy. Show me what I’m doing wrong.”

Onyx grabbed a chair and sat down next to him, reaching for the tools with measured movements.

“Everything is wrong,” he said dryly.

Kairo barked a laugh—exhausted, messy, but real this time.

“Love you too.”


They worked without speaking at first.

Tools switched between hands, quiet clicks and soft metallic taps marking the rhythm. Kairo’s energy, normally feral and restless, thinned into something finer—focused, fragile, determined. The edge of panic melted into precision, and the frantic rush slowed into something steadier.

Onyx angled one of the lamps. “Your power routing is backwards.”

Kairo squinted at it. “No it isn’t—” A beat. “Oh. That’s… that’s backwards.”

“Mhm.”

“Okay but the idea is solid,” Kairo insisted, pointing with a screwdriver as if that made it more scientific. Their shoulders brushed as they leaned over the circuitry. Neither moved away.

Onyx worked with deliberate precision, turning components that had been shoved the wrong way round, adjusting contacts, sliding stabilisers into place. His hands worked like a soldier’s hands, taught to assemble things quickly and correctly, because incorrect things in the field got you killed.

Kairo watched him—the quiet focus, the loosened jaw, the way his shoulders relaxed when he had a task instead of a protocol.

“You really enjoy fixing stuff?” Kairo asked quietly.

Onyx didn’t look up. “I like when things work.”

“You mean people.”

A pause.

Then, quietly:

“Yes.”

A small, aching silence settled between them.

Kairo wiped a hand under his eye, pretending there was something in it. He didn’t think he’d ever felt so tired, but this was important, and he knew that Onyx knew it too. “All right then. Let’s make sure we never lose him again.”

They bent back over the device, their hands moving in sync now: Onyx tightening connections with soldier-steady precision, Kairo tweaking frequencies with instinctive, brilliant speed. The wires looked less frantic now. More like purpose. More like care.

The first test pulse shivered through the board, a soft digital heartbeat, lights flickering uncertainly before holding.

“Okay,” Onyx murmured eventually. “Try the power surge again.”

Kairo connected the leads, breath held without knowing he’d trapped it. The rig gave a cough of static, lights flickering, then—

“Oh,” he breathed. “Oh, it’s working.”

“Not yet,” Onyx said. “But close.”

Kairo turned a dial, fingers trembling just slightly, and the speaker crackled to life. The sound was thin at first, then unexpectedly clear, humming into the space like a lantern switching on in fog.

It sounded like something made out of longing.

Onyx leaned back, listening. “It’s rough,” he said. “Unstable. Distorted.”

“Like us,” Kairo said.

Onyx’s mouth twitched. “…Yeah. Like us.”

The air changed, then, a presence neither of them was expecting.

A voice came from the doorway, quiet enough to break both of them open:

“I’m not going anywhere.”

Kairo jumped a little and turned. Rayne stood just inside the room, barefoot, hair tangled, eyes glowing soft in the low light—caught somewhere between the physical and the signal. 

Kairo froze. “Rayne… how long have you been there?”

The ghost of a smile took shape at the corners of Rayne’s mouth. “Long enough.” He paused, took a tiny step closer. “Thank you for worrying about me.”

Kairo laughed, broken and bright. “Well yeah, obviously—”

And from an adjoining room, Minjae’s voice cut in, sharp and scandalised: “If that thing explodes, I’m deleting both of you!”

“This place is smaller than I realised,” Onyx sighed, but there was warmth in it.

Kairo threw his hands up. “Welcome to the support group for emotionally compromised musicians-slash-rebels. Snacks are not provided.”

A beat. Then laughter. Real. Full. Echoing off concrete.

Rayne came fully into the room and settled down beside them at the table. “Show me how it works.”

As Onyx explained, Kairo settled back in his chair, more relaxed than he’d been in days, because he finally understood:

Sometimes things disappeared for a while. 

But they would always come back.
