Skyline

The Ghost Lines were always coldest above the lower levels.

This hideout was a half-collapsed comms tower, long gutted, with shattered windows and birds nesting in its hollow walls. But it had power, just barely, and a cracked rooftop view of the city skyline, where the neon blinked and the world went on.

Rayne didn’t sleep. Synthetic systems didn’t need it. But sometimes, in the hush between check-ins, he wandered.

He found the sealed room by accident, or maybe instinct. The door had jammed years ago, warped by water damage and rust. It took a precision-angled push and a little trick with his wrist servo to pop the latch.

Inside: ash, fractured steel beams, and silence.

And a terminal.

Not NuYu. It wasn’t encrypted, but it was something old. Civilian-grade.

Rayne moved without thinking, steps weightless over the fractured floor. He crouched in front of the terminal, fingertips brushing dust from the panel.

It blinked.

Flickered once.

Then again.

There was no port or cable, but the interface responded to him.

Rayne froze. This wasn’t normal. He hadn’t authorised a link, but his internal systems pulsed, faint and low like a song remembered from another lifetime. A subroutine he didn’t recognise slid forward and activated without prompt.

[Log playback: confirmed.]

A soft click. Then static.

Then—

A voice.

Faint. Warbled. Singing.

And it was his voice.

Younger. Warmer. Raw. Singing something slow and gentle, off-key in the corners, as if learning as he went. There were no lyrics, just sound.

Then another voice: higher, unfiltered.

“Brother, don’t stop. That part was pretty.”

The log glitched. Blinked. Then—

An image. Fuzzy. Flickering.

A city skyline.

Clear skies. Sunlight.

Rayne reached out to touch the screen as if he could hold the image in his palm.

But then the file cut out.

Silence.


He sat there long after the playback ended, staring at the static-dusted panel.

[Repeat log? Y/N]

He tapped once: Yes.

And again.

And again.

By the fifth playback, his internal diagnostics pinged a soft warning. Memory strain. Recursive loop.

He ignored it.

“This isn’t a system file,” he murmured, barely audible. “Not mine.”

But it was inside him, somehow, fused and embedded, like a memory someone else had handed him to hold.

Behind his eyes—if he had eyes—something flickered. Not a visual. A sensation. Warm light through a window he didn’t recognise. A voice calling his name that wasn’t Rayne, but still made him turn.


The old broadcast room was warmer than the rest of the tower. Someone—probably Kairo—had rerouted the backup cells to power a few overhead lights and a heating coil he’d stolen from a ruined train car. It gave the space a low hum, like the room was purring.

Soahn sat curled near the window, knees tucked in, gaze faraway. Onyx worked silently with a cracked terminal on his lap, screen flickering in and out. Kairo lay on his back with his head near the speaker amp, one leg bouncing to some rhythm only he could hear. Minjae was half-asleep in a ratty chair, hoodie pulled low over his face.

Rayne entered without sound, the small amp in his hands. “I have something.”

They all looked up.

He rarely asked for attention. That alone made them shift and sit straighter.

“I want to play it to you,” he said.

Even Kairo stopped moving.

Rayne crossed to the table and set the speaker down. The soft blue light blinked twice and then connected to the terminal.

He tapped play.


At first it was just static. And then the hum of something ancient.

Then—his voice.

Not the Rayne they knew. Younger. Less precise. Raw and imperfect, as if unused to being heard. Singing wordlessly, the melody trembling under the weight of too much feeling.

The loop wavered, and a child’s voice joined in, laughing between notes:

“Brother, don’t stop. That part was pretty.”

And then, just for a second, a skyline appeared on the terminal screen. Flickering. Impossible. A real sky, endless and clear, like something from a dream. Like something from before.

The log ended.

Silence fell hard.

Kairo sat up slowly, arms resting on his knees. His voice was quiet. “That… that was you, right?”

Rayne stared down at the speaker. “It’s inside me. Buried code. Not system data. Not NuYu firmware.”

Onyx blinked. “You shouldn’t be able to access something like that. Droids aren’t built with preloaded civilian memories.”

“I know.”

Soahn tilted his head. “But you played it.”

Rayne nodded once. “I didn’t choose to store it. It’s not mine.” His voice didn’t shake, but it tightened. “But I remember it anyway.”


Minjae pulled the hoodie off his face. His expression was unreadable—no sarcasm. Just something raw around the edges.

“You sure it’s not… I don’t know. Dev junk? Legacy code?”

Rayne looked up slowly.

“There’s emotion in the audio, but it’s not simulated. It’s human. You can hear it.”

He tapped the terminal again. Let the playback run once more. The melody filled the room like fog.

Kairo’s fingers twitched against his knee.

Soahn stood and crossed the space without a word. He rested a hand, just briefly, on Rayne’s shoulder.

“Keep it,” he said.

Rayne’s mouth tightened at the corners. “It doesn’t belong to me.”

Soahn’s voice didn’t waver. “It does now.”


Kairo stood too, slower, brow furrowed. “This was an intentional embed. Some early model devs… they hardwired memory logs into emotional AI prototypes. Thought it’d make them feel more real. Most of that stuff got purged.”

Soahn added, softly, staring up at Rayne, “Yours wasn’t.”

Rayne nodded, once. “Someone must’ve wanted me to carry it.”

Minjae exhaled, long and low. “Damn. You really are the tragic one.”

Kairo gave a dry smile. “Brooding. Beautiful. Secretly sentimental. It’s a lot. I’m overwhelmed.”

Rayne didn’t smile, but something in his posture eased. “You’re welcome.”


Soahn reached for the speaker and turned the volume down slightly. The voice faded, but the feeling remained.

“I think,” he said slowly, “someone gave you a memory so they wouldn’t be forgotten. And now… they won’t be.”

Rayne sat down and folded his hands in his lap. The city beyond the tower windows glitched and flickered in the light.

No one said anything else.

They didn’t have to.


They stayed like that for a while. No one really knew what to say, so they didn’t try.

The city outside the tower buzzed in the distance, just far enough away for them to feel safe for that moment.

Minjae broke the silence first, not with a joke, but something softer. “Do you think he was real? The kid?”

Rayne shook his head. “I don’t know.”

“Feels real,” Kairo murmured. “The laughter. That kind of joy… you can’t code that. It bleeds.”

Rayne’s hand curled slightly around the amp. Something about that laugh—it lived in him like heat. A flicker with no source.

Onyx crossed his arms, gaze fixed on the amp. “Does it matter if it’s real? Someone gave it to you.”

Rayne turned to him.

“Why?”

Onyx didn’t flinch. “Maybe they couldn’t hold it anymore.”

Soahn spoke without looking up. “Maybe they built you to carry grief.”

Minjae huffed. “That’s dark.”

“It’s true,” Soahn said. “Some memories are too heavy. So people give them away. That doesn’t make them lighter, just… spread out.”

Rayne looked back at the speaker. “I shouldn’t be affected by this,” he said.

Minjae leaned his chin into his hand. “Yeah, well, I shouldn’t cry at bootleg anime dubs, but here we are.”

Kairo shifted closer, nodding toward the speaker. “We could use it.”

Rayne blinked. “What?”

“The melody. That note. The skyline flash. We could loop it. Sample your voice. Make it into a track.” He grinned faintly. “We’ve been looking for Signal 20. Maybe this is it.”

Rayne hesitated. “It doesn’t feel like mine.”

Soahn looked at him, more intensely than usual. “It is now.”

“That’s how memories work,” Minjae added, quieter than usual, “Doesn’t matter who they started with. What matters is who carries them forward.”

The amp blinked once—a final pulse—and powered down.

Rayne reached over and pulled it close, wrapping the cables around it with precise, careful movements.

Kairo gave a small, one-shoulder shrug. “No pressure. But if you ever want to finish that song…” He tapped his temple. “I’ve already got the beat.”

Rayne looked up. “Would you build it around silence?”

“Always,” Kairo said. “That’s where the best parts hide.”


That night, Rayne didn’t wander. Instead, he sat with them.

Let the conversation drift around him, like warm light. Let himself be part of the moment, not outside it.

He didn’t remember the skyline. But he remembered the feeling.

And somehow, that was enough.
